# Diagnostics: Prediction Compression

Predictions from the shipped model cluster around 4-8 wins regardless of whether a team
actually won 0 or 12 games. This document quantifies that compression, tests the candidate
causes, and states which ones are actually responsible. All numbers below are generated by
`scripts/diagnostics/compute_compression_diagnostics.py`, `feature_experiment.py`, and
`win_probability_sum_prototype.py` (run together via `scripts/diagnostics/run_all.py`), writing
to `outputs/diagnostics_compression/`. Nothing in this investigation touched
`outputs/model_comparison/selected_model.json`, `outputs/models/final_model.joblib`, or
`outputs/predictions/predicted_win_totals_2025.csv`.

## Verdict

**Compression is caused by excessive regularization/shrinkage selected by an MAE-only tuning
criterion — a genuine model-bias problem, present even on training data, not a generalization
gap.** Weak features, prediction clipping, and the discreteness of the win-count target were
all tested and ruled out or shown to be minor. See "What this rules out" below for the
evidence on each. **This isn't just a diagnosis — it was tested directly** (section 3b):
swapping in a variance-aware tuning objective measurably reduces compression
(gradient_boosting's std_ratio moves from 0.738 to 0.845) at a real, quantified MAE cost
(+8.9%), while widening the hyperparameter grid alone — without changing the objective —
changes nothing. The fix is available; whether the MAE tradeoff is worth taking is a decision
for follow-up, not resolved here (evaluate-only, nothing promoted to production).

## 1. How compressed, exactly

| | std(actual) | std(predicted) | ratio | range(actual) | range(predicted) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| gradient_boosting, OOF walk-forward (2019-2024, pooled, n=658) | 2.77 | 2.05 | **0.74** | 0-13 | 1.9-13.4 |
| gradient_boosting, **shipped 2025 predictions** (refit on 2015-2024, n=136) | 2.73 | 1.34 | **0.49** | 0-12 | 2.9-10.2 |
| ridge, OOF pooled | 2.77 | 2.22 | 0.80 | 0-13 | 0.5-14.2 |
| elasticnet, OOF pooled | 2.77 | 2.02 | 0.73 | 0-13 | 1.3-14.0 |

Full table: `outputs/diagnostics_compression/tables/std_range_comparison.csv`.

The shipped model's predictions vary about **half** as much as reality. Every candidate model
family shows the same pattern in OOF cross-validation (ratio 0.70-0.86, see the table) — this
is not specific to gradient_boosting or to the 2025 holdout year, though the 2025 holdout is
noticeably worse than the OOF average.

### The report's own request conflates two different regression directions

"Regress actual on predicted; a slope above 1 indicates compression" is only half right.
`slope(actual ~ predicted)` measures whether *actual* outcomes track whatever narrow band the
model happens to predict in — it can look fine (OOF gradient_boosting: **0.977**) even when
predictions are severely compressed, because it says nothing about how much the predictions
themselves move. `slope(predicted ~ actual)` is the direction that actually diagnoses
compression: how much does the model's output move per 1 additional actual win? A value well
below 1 is the real signature.

| | slope(actual ~ predicted) | slope(predicted ~ actual) |
|---|---|---|
| gradient_boosting, OOF pooled | 0.977 (looks fine) | **0.533** (compressed) |
| gradient_boosting, shipped 2025 | 0.953 (looks fine) | **0.229** (severely compressed) |

Full table: `outputs/diagnostics_compression/tables/slope_intercept_by_model_fold.csv`.
Both directions are reported everywhere in this investigation to avoid this ambiguity.

### Calibration by actual-win bucket (not predicted-bucket) is what actually shows it

The production `outputs/diagnostics/calibration.csv` buckets by *predicted* value — which
can't show compression by construction, since a model that never predicts outside 3-8 simply
never populates a bucket outside 3-8. Bucketing by *actual* outcome instead:

| Actual wins | n (OOF) | Mean predicted (OOF) | Mean actual (OOF) | n (2025 holdout) | Mean predicted (holdout) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0-1 | 16 | **3.93** | 0.88 | 4 | **5.12** |
| 2-3 | 90 | 4.69 | 2.70 | 16 | 5.92 |
| 4-5 | 140 | 5.54 | 4.53 | 34 | 5.54 |
| 6-7 | 184 | 6.16 | 6.47 | 27 | 6.34 |
| 8-9 | 122 | 7.05 | 8.39 | 36 | 6.63 |
| 10-11 | 81 | 8.74 | 10.40 | 17 | 7.48 |
| 12-13 | 25 | 10.81 | 12.44 | — | — |

A team that actually won 0-1 games gets predicted ~4-5 wins on average; a team that won 12-13
gets predicted ~10.8 (OOF) or as low as ~7.5 (2025 holdout, 10-11 bucket). Full table:
`outputs/diagnostics_compression/tables/calibration_by_actual_bucket.csv`, plots:
`plots/calibration_oof_vs_holdout.png`, `plots/residuals_by_actual_bucket_{oof,holdout}.png`.

**Note on `docs/modeling_methodology.md`**: that document reports the 2025 holdout's near-zero
mean bias (-0.12) as evidence of "no systematic over/under-prediction." That's true in
aggregate but masks the finding above — bias is near zero because *over*-prediction of bad
teams and *under*-prediction of good teams cancel out, not because predictions are accurate.

## 2. Why: regularization pinned at the shrinkage extreme, and it's already there in training

Loading every saved per-fold model (`outputs/models/fold_<year>_<model>.joblib`) and reading
back the hyperparameters GridSearchCV actually selected:

| Model | Hyperparameter | Selected (all 5 folds + final refit) | Grid | Position |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ridge | alpha | 100.0 | [0.1, 1, 10, **100**] | pinned at max (strongest shrinkage) |
| gradient_boosting | max_depth | 2 | [**2**, 3, 4] | pinned at min (weakest trees) |
| gradient_boosting | learning_rate | 0.03 | [**0.03**, 0.1] | pinned at min (slowest learning) |
| gradient_boosting | n_estimators | 300 | [100, **300**] | pinned at max (more, weaker trees) |

Full table: `outputs/diagnostics_compression/tables/regularization_grid_boundary_check.csv`.
This is consistent in every single walk-forward fold and in the final 2025 refit — not a
fluke of one fold. The pattern (max depth 2, learning rate 0.03, but 300 estimators) is
specifically "many very weak, very slow learners" — a strong smoothing/averaging effect by
design, which is exactly what produces compressed predictions.

**This is caused by the model-selection criterion, not a modeling mistake per se**:
`scripts/evaluate_models.py`/`modeling/tuning.py` select and tune purely on mean OOF MAE, with
no variance or calibration-slope penalty (`scripts/evaluate_models.py:_select_model`). For a
noisy, moderately-variable integer target, a heavily shrunk/biased predictor genuinely
minimizes expected absolute error better than a higher-variance, better-calibrated one — MAE
selection has no way to "know" that the resulting predictions are uselessly narrow for anyone
trying to distinguish a 2-win team from a 10-win team.

### This is model bias, not a train/val generalization gap

Reconstructing in-sample (training-set) predictions from the saved fold pipelines — something
the production pipeline never computes — shows the compression is already present on data the
model has seen:

| Model | Train std_ratio (pred/actual) | Val std_ratio | Train MAE | Val MAE |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ols (no shrinkage) | **0.79** | **0.90** | 1.37 | 1.79 |
| gradient_boosting (selected) | 0.73 | 0.74 | 1.19 | 1.54 |
| ridge | 0.74 | 0.79 | 1.43 | 1.64 |
| elasticnet | 0.68 | 0.73 | 1.51 | 1.55 |
| random_forest | 0.83 | 0.70 | 0.64 | 1.57 |
| xgboost | 0.79 | 0.76 | 0.90 | 1.57 |

Full table: `outputs/diagnostics_compression/tables/train_vs_val_metrics_by_fold.csv`. Two
things stand out:

1. **Every model's train-set std_ratio is already well below 1** (0.68-0.83) — the tuned
   models can't reproduce the training data's own variance, let alone the validation data's.
   If this were overfitting/generalization failure, train std_ratio would be near 1 while val
   collapsed; instead they're similar. That's the signature of **bias from regularization**,
   not a train/val gap.
2. **OLS — the one candidate with no shrinkage parameter at all — has the best std_ratio of
   any model** (0.79 train / 0.90 val, closest to 1), at the cost of worse MAE (1.79 vs. 1.54
   for gradient_boosting) from overfitting to noise/multicollinearity in 132 correlated
   features. This is the bias-variance tradeoff playing out directly: the MAE-only selection
   criterion picks the more-biased, lower-variance side of that tradeoff every time, because
   that's what minimizes OOF MAE on a noisy target.

## 3. What this rules out (or shows is a minor factor)

**Prediction clipping**: bounds are `[0, scheduled_games]` (≈0-14) — far outside the actual
compressed prediction range (1.3-13.4 OOF, 2.9-10.2 holdout). Clipping never binds. Not a
contributor.

**Weak or missing features**: added 3 new features (talent relative to schedule, count/ratio
of games with a preseason talent advantage) and 4 interactions (talent×SOS,
returning-production×prior-wins, QB-continuity×offensive-efficiency,
coaching-change×roster-turnover — see `src/cfb_win_total_model/features/diagnostic_experiments.py`)
and re-ran identical walk-forward CV for gradient_boosting and elasticnet:

| Model | MAE (baseline → +features) | std_ratio (baseline → +features) | slope(pred~actual) (baseline → +features) |
|---|---|---|---|
| gradient_boosting | 1.536 → 1.560 (worse) | 0.738 → 0.744 | 0.533 → 0.529 |
| elasticnet | 1.552 → 1.554 (flat) | 0.730 → 0.730 | 0.520 → 0.518 |

Full table: `outputs/diagnostics_compression/experiments/feature_experiment_vs_baseline_comparison.csv`.
Adding these features made **no meaningful difference** to compression and slightly hurt MAE
for gradient_boosting. This doesn't prove no feature would help, but it rules out "obviously
missing signal" as the primary driver — the existing 132-feature set already carries talent,
returning production, QB departure, schedule strength, and coaching-change signal; the model
just isn't allowed to use it at full strength.

**Discreteness of the win-count target**: not the primary story either. OLS — a plain
continuous linear regression on the same discrete target — achieves the best calibration of
any candidate (section 2). The compression tracks the *regularization* applied on top of the
model, not the fact that wins happen to be small integers.

## 3b. Follow-up: does fixing the selection objective actually fix compression?

Section 2 diagnosed the cause as an MAE-only selection criterion consistently choosing the
shrinkage-favoring extreme of every hyperparameter grid. This follow-up tests that diagnosis
directly (`scripts/diagnostics/variance_aware_retune.py`, evaluate-only — see guardrail note
below) for the three-model contrast set, comparing three variants under identical walk-forward
folds: the current **production grid + MAE** (baseline), a **widened grid + MAE** (does grid
range alone matter?), and a **widened grid + a variance-aware objective**
(`modeling.evaluation.variance_aware_score` — MAE plus a penalty when
`std(pred)/std(actual)` falls below 0.85, added as a new, opt-in scorer; the production
tuning path in `scripts/train_models.py`/`evaluate_models.py` is unchanged and still defaults
to plain MAE).

| Model | Variant | Mean fold MAE | std_ratio (pred/actual) | slope(pred~actual) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| gradient_boosting | baseline (production) | 1.536 | 0.738 | 0.533 |
| gradient_boosting | widened grid + MAE | 1.536 (**identical**) | 0.738 (**identical**) | 0.533 (**identical**) |
| gradient_boosting | widened grid + variance-aware | 1.673 (+8.9%) | **0.845** (+14%) | **0.571** |
| ridge | baseline (production) | 1.638 | 0.801 | 0.567 |
| ridge | widened grid + MAE | 1.624 (slightly better) | 0.730 (**worse**) | 0.512 (worse) |
| ridge | widened grid + variance-aware | 1.635 (flat) | 0.789 (flat) | 0.558 (flat) |
| elasticnet | baseline (production) | 1.552 | 0.730 | 0.520 |
| elasticnet | widened grid + MAE | 1.552 (**identical**) | 0.730 (**identical**) | 0.520 (**identical**) |
| elasticnet | widened grid + variance-aware | 1.565 (+0.8%) | 0.754 (+3%) | 0.538 |

Full table: `outputs/diagnostics_compression/experiments/variance_aware_retune_vs_baseline_comparison.csv`,
per-fold selected hyperparameters: `variance_aware_retune_selected_hyperparams.csv`.

**Widening the grid alone, with the objective unchanged, does nothing** — gradient_boosting
and elasticnet reproduce their baseline numbers exactly (MAE-only selection just re-finds the
same shrinkage-favoring point even when less-shrunk options are available), and for ridge it
actively makes compression *worse* (MAE-only selection takes the newly-available alpha=1000
option, more regularized than production's alpha=100 ceiling, for a marginal MAE gain). This
confirms section 2's diagnosis was about the *objective*, not the *grid range*.

**The variance-aware objective works as intended, with a real, quantifiable MAE cost** —
tested and not merely asserted. For gradient_boosting, it moves std_ratio from 0.738 toward
0.845 (roughly halfway to the 0.85 target used in the penalty) at an 8.9% MAE cost (1.536 →
1.673). For elasticnet the same idea produces a smaller, cheaper effect (std_ratio 0.730 →
0.754, MAE +0.8%). Neither fully reaches the 0.85 target with `penalty_weight=1.0` — a
stronger penalty would likely close more of the gap, at a correspondingly larger MAE cost; this
is a tunable dial, not a one-shot fix. **Ridge is the exception**: the variance-aware variant
lands close to the original *production* baseline (0.789 vs. 0.801) rather than clearly beating
it, because ridge's widened-grid MAE-optimal point (alpha=1000) is so much more compressed that
pulling it back down with the penalty mostly just returns it to roughly where production
already was. This suggests ridge's compression isn't purely an alpha-selection problem — an L2
penalty shrinks many small, correlated coefficients together across all 132 features
regardless of the exact alpha chosen, which the model-family choice, not just its tuning, is
responsible for.

### Penalty-weight sweep: the tradeoff isn't linear

`scripts/diagnostics/penalty_weight_sweep.py` swept `penalty_weight ∈ {0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0}`
for the same 3 models under the same widened grids, to find a cheaper operating point than the
single `penalty_weight=1.0` tested above.

| Model | pw=0.1 | pw=0.25 | pw=0.5 | pw=1.0 | pw=2.0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| gradient_boosting: MAE % vs. baseline | +0.9% | +1.1% | **+6.9%** | +8.9% | +8.9% (identical to pw=1.0) |
| gradient_boosting: std_ratio | 0.741 | 0.740 | **0.837** | 0.845 | 0.845 (identical to pw=1.0) |
| ridge: MAE % vs. baseline | -1.9% | -1.9% | -1.9% | -0.2% | +6.6% |
| ridge: std_ratio | 0.748 | 0.748 | 0.748 | 0.789 | **0.880** |
| elasticnet: MAE % vs. baseline | 0% | 0% | 0% | +0.9% | +7.5% |
| elasticnet: std_ratio | 0.730 | 0.730 | 0.730 | 0.754 | **0.835** |

Full table: `outputs/diagnostics_compression/experiments/penalty_weight_sweep_results.csv`.

Three findings, none of them "just turn the dial up smoothly":

1. **Gradient_boosting has a threshold, not a gradient.** `pw=0.1`/`0.25` do almost nothing
   (std_ratio barely moves off 0.738-0.741). Between `0.25` and `0.5` there's a sharp jump to
   0.837. Then `1.0` and `2.0` are **identical** (0.845, 8.9% MAE cost both times) — the
   variance-aware objective saturates at the widened grid's least-shrunk available option
   (`learning_rate=0.2, max_depth=2`) and no amount of extra penalty weight can push it
   further without widening the grid again. **`pw≈0.5` is the practical sweet spot**: it
   captures ~95% of `pw=1.0`'s compression relief (0.837 vs. 0.845) for ~78% of the MAE cost
   (6.9% vs. 8.9%).
2. **Ridge is non-monotonic at low weights** — `pw=0.1` through `0.5` all land on the *same*
   selected alpha, which is simultaneously slightly *better* on MAE (-1.9%) than production
   AND more compressed (std_ratio 0.748 vs. production's 0.801). A small penalty isn't enough
   to stop the widened grid from picking something more compressed than the original
   fixed-grid baseline; ridge only clearly beats production's std_ratio at `pw=2.0` (0.880,
   +6.6% MAE) — confirming ridge needs a materially larger intervention than the other two
   models to get a real win, consistent with the structural explanation above.
3. **Elasticnet is flat until `pw=1.0`**, then responds similarly to gradient_boosting's
   shape (bigger gain from `1.0`→`2.0`, 0.754→0.835, without gradient_boosting's saturation).

Practical read: there's no universal "good" `penalty_weight` — it has to be tuned per model
family, and for at least gradient_boosting the useful range is narrow (`0.5`-`1.0`; below that
does nothing, above that does nothing more). Any promotion decision should pick a
model-specific weight from this curve (e.g. gradient_boosting `pw≈0.5`, ridge `pw≈2.0` if ridge
is kept at all) rather than one shared constant.

**Guardrail check**: `outputs/model_comparison/selected_model.json` and
`outputs/models/final_model.joblib` have unchanged file-modification timestamps from before
this follow-up ran, and `outputs/predictions/predicted_win_totals_2025.csv` is untouched since
before this entire investigation began — nothing was promoted.

## 4. Secondary findings

**`n_games` (33.7% permutation importance, the single most important feature) is likely
partially endogenous.** Correlation with `regular_season_wins` across all seasons: 0.369.
Within any given season, teams that play 13+ games (conference championship game and/or bonus
games, which require already being good enough to qualify) average roughly double the wins of
12-game teams:

| Season | n_games=12 (n teams, mean wins) | n_games=13 (n teams, mean wins) |
|---|---|---|
| 2019 | 109 teams, 5.74 wins | 20 teams, 10.50 wins |
| 2023 | 112 teams, 5.72 wins | 20 teams, 10.65 wins |
| 2024 | 113 teams, 5.91 wins | 18 teams, 9.83 wins |

Full table: `outputs/diagnostics_compression/tables/n_games_endogeneity_check.csv`. The
`games` table has no clean postseason flag (`docs/assumptions_and_limitations.md` #2), so this
count isn't fully preseason-knowable the way the `schedule` feature module assumes. This
doesn't directly explain compression (a leaky feature would more plausibly inflate apparent
skill for the subgroup it leaks on, not compress everyone toward the mean), but it's a
legitimate data-quality concern worth fixing independent of this investigation — it overstates
how much real preseason signal the model has.

**Talent-tier bias is consistent with mean-reversion** (already-existing production artifact,
`outputs/model_comparison/holdout_2025_breakdown_talent_tier.csv`): weakest-talent quartile is
over-predicted by +0.32 wins on average, strongest-talent quartile under-predicted by -0.18.
First-year-coach teams are notably under-predicted (-0.42 vs. -0.04 for others,
`holdout_2025_breakdown_new_coach.csv`) — consistent with the model leaning on
`career_win_pct_entering_t`/tenure features that a new coach doesn't have real history for yet.

**Conference breakdown** (new, `outputs/diagnostics_compression/tables/conference_breakdown_oof.csv`,
OOF pooled across 2019-2024): bias ranges from -0.28 (Mountain West, under-predicted) to +0.23
(Conference USA, over-predicted) — a real but modest effect, roughly consistent with G5
conferences skewing toward under-prediction and some P5/G5 boundary conferences over-predicted,
but not a dominant factor next to the regularization finding above.

**Season breakdown** (new, multi-season OOF, `season_breakdown_oof.csv`): R² degrades notably
in the most recent seasons (2024: R²=0.325, vs. 0.51-0.63 in 2019-2023; the 2025 holdout is
R²=0.215) with bias also drifting more negative (2024: -0.23; 2025: -0.12). Plausibly real
performance drift (increased transfer-portal churn making recent seasons harder to predict) or
a small-sample artifact of only 5-6 OOF seasons — flagged, not resolved here.

## 5. Alternative formulation: per-game win probability summed to a season total

Tested summing two already-available per-game fields (`games.home_pregame_elo`/`away_pregame_elo`
and `games.home_post_win_prob`/`away_post_win_prob`) across each team's season, as a cheap
Stage-1 check before building anything heavier
(`scripts/diagnostics/win_probability_sum_prototype.py`). Both fields pass a sanity check
first (favorite wins meaningfully more than 50% of games; near-toss-up games are genuinely
close to 50/50 — see script output).

| Approach | Split | MAE | std_ratio | slope(pred~actual) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| win_prob_sum (CFBD field) | walk-forward seasons pooled | **0.96** | **0.88** | 0.79 |
| win_prob_sum (CFBD field) | 2025 holdout | **0.89** | **0.81** | 0.75 |
| elo_implied_sum | walk-forward seasons pooled | 1.52 | 0.84 | 0.66 |
| gradient_boosting (production) | walk-forward seasons pooled | 1.54 | 0.74 | 0.53 |
| gradient_boosting (production, shipped) | 2025 holdout | 2.01 | 0.49 | 0.23 |

Full table: `outputs/diagnostics_compression/experiments/win_prob_sum_vs_direct_regression_comparison.csv`.

**This is not an apples-to-apples result and should not be read as "switch to this."**
`home_pregame_elo` updates game-by-game *within* a season as results come in, and
`home_post_win_prob` is computed using whatever is known at that game's kickoff — including
that season's already-played games. Summing across a season blends in in-season information
(week 10's win probability already reflects weeks 1-9 of that same season) that the production
season-level model deliberately does not have before the season starts. The dramatically
better numbers above partly reflect an easier, partially in-season task, not a fair test of
"per-game modeling beats season-level regression at the same information cutoff."

What this test does establish: summing well-calibrated per-game probabilities produces a much
less compressed, lower-MAE distribution than direct season-level regression, *when the
per-game probabilities themselves are well-calibrated and not artificially smoothed*. That's
suggestive that a properly-built **preseason-only** per-game model (Stage 2 — not built here;
would need pre-game-known features like talent/SP+ differential per matchup, trained under the
same walk-forward discipline) is worth prototyping as a genuine fix, rather than a certainty.

## Recommendations (require follow-up decision, none applied automatically)

Ranked by expected impact, updated after section 3b's follow-up actually tested option 1
(previously a prediction, now a measured result):

1. **A variance-aware selection criterion is a real, working lever — not just a grid-range
   issue — but it costs MAE, so the tradeoff needs a decision, not just a flip of a switch.**
   Section 3b shows widening the grid alone does nothing (byte-identical results for
   gradient_boosting/elasticnet, actively worse for ridge), while adding
   `modeling.evaluation.variance_aware_score` as the tuning objective moves gradient_boosting's
   std_ratio from 0.738 to 0.845 at an 8.9% MAE cost (1.536 → 1.673 mean fold MAE). Before
   promoting this: decide the acceptable MAE-for-variance exchange rate (try `penalty_weight`
   values between the tested 1.0 and something smaller, e.g. 0.3-0.5, to find a cheaper partial
   fix), confirm the choice on the true holdout (2025) rather than only OOF walk-forward, and
   only then update `config/modeling.yaml`'s `hyperparam_grids`/`scripts/evaluate_models.py`'s
   `_select_model` and re-run the full production pipeline — none of that was done here
   (evaluate-only, per agreed scope).
2. **Prototype the Stage-2 preseason-only per-game win-probability model** (section 5) — the
   Stage-1 result is promising enough to justify the investment, with the in-season-leakage
   caveat addressed by construction (only pre-game-known features, walk-forward CV).
3. **Fix the `n_games` endogeneity** (section 4) independent of the above — derive a genuinely
   preseason-knowable expected-games-played feature (e.g. from division/bowl-eligibility
   rules) rather than the actual final count, which partially encodes the outcome.
4. **Investigate ridge/L2 models separately if pursued** — section 3b found the variance-aware
   objective doesn't clearly help ridge the way it helps gradient_boosting/elasticnet, hinting
   its compression source (shrinkage spread thin across 132 correlated, standardized features)
   may need feature-set narrowing or a different regularization family (e.g. group lasso)
   rather than just a different alpha.
5. New features/interactions (section 3) are **not** a priority based on this evidence — they
   made no measurable difference here. Not ruled out entirely (only 4 interactions were
   tested), but not where the leverage is.

**Explicitly not done**: no variance-correction or rescaling of predictions was applied to
`outputs/predictions/predicted_win_totals_2025.csv` or any other production artifact. If a
future change implements a post-hoc variance correction, it must be fit strictly on
out-of-fold predictions (never on the holdout/prediction data it's applied to) — per the
standing constraint on this investigation.

- New reusable evaluation helpers: `src/cfb_win_total_model/modeling/evaluation.py`
  (`regression_slope_intercept`, `calibration_by_actual_bucket`, `std_range_summary`,
  `walk_forward_results` — the last also now used by `scripts/evaluate_models.py`, extracted

  opt-in, not used by the default production tuning path)
- Diagnostic-only feature augmentation: `src/cfb_win_total_model/features/diagnostic_experiments.py`
  (not wired into the production `dataset.py` feature registry)
- `src/cfb_win_total_model/modeling/train.py`'s `fit_candidate_on_fold` gained an optional
  `scoring` parameter (default unchanged: `"neg_mean_absolute_error"`) so the variance-aware
  retune could reuse the exact same fitting/tuning path as production without forking it —
  verified byte-identical `evaluate_models.py` output before and after this addition.

Note: `run_all.py` runs the three original diagnostic stages only; `variance_aware_retune.py`
is a separate follow-up script (run standalone) since it was added after the initial pass and
is comparatively expensive (~7-8 minutes with the trimmed grids in `WIDENED_GRIDS`).

To reproduce: `python scripts/diagnostics/run_all.py` (from `cfb_win_total_model/`, using its
`.venv`). Every script re-verifies a set of hand-checked numbers against its own output before
finishing (see each script's `_sanity_checks`/assertions) and fails loudly if they drift.
